Type A — Chapter Hero FIG 1.2-A The Relay — Electricity Controlling Electricity
A telegraph relay in cross-section. Left: the 5V control circuit contains the electromagnetic coil. When current flows through it, the coil becomes a magnet. Centre: the iron armature is pulled down against the spring tension, closing the gap between the two contact points. Right: this closure completes the 48V output circuit, powering the load. The relay is electrically isolated — the control and output circuits never touch. A 5mA control signal switches a 2A output: this is both amplification and binary switching.
Type E — Before vs After FIG 1.2-B Relay States — Deenergised (0) vs Energised (1)
The two states of a normally-open relay. Deenergised (left): no current in the coil — no magnetic field — spring holds the armature away from the fixed contact — gap remains open — output circuit broken — logic 0. Energised (right): current in the coil creates a magnetic field — field pulls the iron armature down against spring tension — moving contact touches fixed contact — output circuit completes — logic 1. This is the fundamental binary switching action that relay computers of the 1940s scaled to thousands of units.
Type B — Historical Reconstruction FIG 1.2-C Telegraph Relay Station — Signal Regeneration, 1840s
Telegraph relay stations (1840s) were the first practical application of the relay as a computing device. A signal sent from New York arrives in Philadelphia as a weak, attenuated pulse. The relay station uses this weak signal to trigger a relay, which uses local battery power to regenerate a full-strength signal for the next leg to Washington. Claude Shannon's 1937 MIT thesis showed that this regeneration logic — relay networks making decisions — is mathematically equivalent to Boolean algebra. The telegraph offices had been running digital circuits for 90 years without knowing it.
Type C — Concept Diagram FIG 1.2-D The Switch as a Binary Device — Every Switch in History
The switch abstraction across six generations. Manual toggle (1800s), electromagnetic relay (1835), vacuum tube triode (1906), bipolar transistor (1947), CMOS logic gate (1963), 5nm FinFET (2011). Each is controlled by a different physical mechanism — mechanical force, electrical current, electron emission, semiconductor doping, voltage — but every one presents identical Boolean behaviour: exactly two states. This substrate-independence is what makes digital logic timeless.
