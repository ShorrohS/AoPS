Type A — Chapter Hero FIG 1.3-A AND, OR, NOT — Three Relay Circuits, Three Logical Operations
The three fundamental relay logic circuits. AND gate (left): two relays in series — both A and B must be energised for current to reach the output. Truth table shows output is 1 only when A=1 AND B=1. OR gate (centre): two relays in parallel — either path can complete the circuit. Output is 1 when A=1 OR B=1 (or both). NOT gate (right): a normally-closed relay — the contact is closed by default and opens when the coil is energised, inverting the input. These three operations are the complete basis of all digital logic.
Type C — Concept Diagram FIG 1.3-B Relay Half Adder — Adding Two Bits with Metal and Wire
A relay half adder built from AND, OR, and NOT relay circuits. SUM = A XOR B (computed as (A OR B) AND NOT(A AND B)). CARRY = A AND B. The critical insight from the truth table: 1+1 in binary produces SUM=0 and CARRY=1 — the carry represents the overflow into the next column. Chaining two half adders with an OR gate for carries produces a full adder, which is the foundation of every arithmetic unit ever built.
Type C — Concept Diagram FIG 1.3-C Shannon 1937 — Circuits Are Boolean Algebra
Claude Shannon's 1937 master's thesis established that relay switching circuits implement Boolean algebra. Series relay contacts correspond to the AND operation. Parallel paths correspond to OR. Normally-closed contacts correspond to NOT. This means any Boolean expression can be directly translated into a relay circuit, and any relay circuit can be analysed and minimised using Boolean algebra. This thesis — submitted when Shannon was 21 — is arguably the most important master's thesis ever written, because it gave engineers a mathematical language to design digital systems.
