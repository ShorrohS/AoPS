Type A — Chapter Hero FIG 1.5-A The NPN Transistor as a Switch — Base Controls Collector-Emitter
NPN bipolar transistor used as a digital switch. The base terminal (B) receives a small control current through a resistor. When base voltage is low (0V), no base current flows, the transistor is in cutoff, and the collector-emitter path is an open circuit — load off, output 0. When base voltage is high (5V), a small base current (microamps) flows, forward-biasing the base-emitter junction, which collapses the depletion region and allows a large collector current (milliamps) to flow through the load — output 1. The transistor amplifies current while acting as a perfect binary switch, with no vacuum, no heater, and no mechanical parts.
Type E — Comparison FIG 1.5-B Relay → Vacuum Tube → Transistor — The Switching Technology Progression
Three generations of electronic switching technology. The relay (1835) uses electromagnetism to move a mechanical contact — slow, large, reliable. The vacuum tube (1906) eliminates mechanical movement using electron emission in vacuum — 100× faster but fragile, power-hungry, bulky. The transistor (1947) uses semiconductor junctions — no vacuum, no heater, no moving parts, inherently scalable. Each generation transitions from a physical phenomenon (mechanical, thermionic, quantum-mechanical tunnelling) to a purely solid-state operation. The transistor's scalability is the critical property that enables Moore's Law.
Type B — Concept Diagram FIG 1.5-C MOSFET Cross-Section — Gate Voltage Controls the Channel
MOSFET cross-section — the transistor architecture that powers all modern chips. When gate voltage exceeds the threshold (Vth), an inversion layer of electrons forms in the P-type silicon beneath the ultra-thin gate oxide, creating a conducting channel between source and drain. Below Vth, no channel exists: the transistor is off. The gate oxide thickness (tox) is the critical dimension scaled with each process node, now approaching just a few atomic layers — at which point quantum tunnelling causes leakage current even when the gate is off.
Type D — Simulation Figure FIG 1.5-D From Discrete Transistors to Integrated Circuits — Bell Labs 1947 to Apple M3 2023
The integration revolution from Bell Labs to modern VLSI. Jack Kilby's 1958 integrated circuit (Nobel Prize 2000) was the pivotal insight: instead of wiring together discrete components, etch all of them onto a single piece of semiconductor. This made manufacturing complexity essentially free — a chip with one transistor and one with a billion cost similar amounts to fabricate. The Apple M3 (2023) contains 25 billion transistors on a die smaller than a postage stamp, all operating within a 22W thermal envelope.
