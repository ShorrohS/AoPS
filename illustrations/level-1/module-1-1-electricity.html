Module 1.1 — What Is Electricity?
4 figures · Chapter Hero · Water Analogy · Circuit States · Voltage/Current Concept
Type A — Chapter Hero FIG 1.1-A Electricity as Controlled Flow — The Foundation of All Computing
A complete series circuit: a 9V battery, connecting wires, and an incandescent bulb. Red dots show electron flow direction (negative to positive terminal). Current flows continuously as long as the circuit is unbroken. The three core quantities — voltage (driving force), current (flow rate), and resistance (opposition) — are related by Ohm's Law: V = I × R. For computing, the key insight is that voltage levels can represent binary states: high voltage = 1, low voltage = 0.
Type C — Concept Diagram FIG 1.1-B The Water Analogy — Voltage, Current, and Resistance Made Intuitive
The water analogy for electricity. Left: a plumbing system — reservoir (high pressure), valve (switch), narrow pipe (resistance), low basin, and pump (battery) returning water. Right: the electrical equivalent. Centre: the analogy map. The critical computing insight is the valve/switch row: an open valve allows flow (circuit ON), a closed valve blocks it (circuit OFF). A transistor is a voltage-controlled valve — the fundamental building block of all digital logic.
Type E — Before vs After FIG 1.1-C Open Circuit vs Closed Circuit — The Binary Reality of Switches
The two states of a switch map directly to the two binary values. Open circuit: the switch creates a gap, breaking the conducting path. No current flows. The bulb is dark. This represents logic 0 / LOW. Closed circuit: the switch connects the gap, completing the path. Current flows through the filament, producing light. This represents logic 1 / HIGH. Every bit in every computer is physically this — a switch (transistor) that is either open or closed.
Type F — Simulation Figure FIG 1.1-D Series vs Parallel Circuits — Two Bulbs, Two Behaviours
Series vs parallel circuits — the simulation's second exercise. Series (left): both bulbs share the same current path. Each gets half the voltage, so both glow dimly. Remove one bulb and both go out — a cascade failure. Parallel (right): each bulb has its own independent path to the battery. Each receives the full 9V and glows at full brightness. Remove one bulb and the other stays on. CPU power delivery uses massive parallel distribution for exactly this reason: independent, redundant paths.
