Module 1.4 — Vacuum Tubes: Faster Switching

Type A — Chapter Hero FIG 1.4-A The Triode Vacuum Tube — Electrons Controlled by a Grid
Triode vacuum tube anatomy. The heated cathode emits a cloud of electrons by thermionic emission. The control grid, positioned between cathode and anode, acts as an electrostatic gate: a negative grid voltage repels electrons, blocking current (tube OFF = logic 0). A zero or positive grid voltage allows electrons to stream through to the anode (tube ON = logic 1). The switching action is purely electronic — no moving parts — making the triode approximately 100× faster than an electromagnetic relay. ENIAC (1945) used 18,000 of these to reach 5,000 operations per second.
Type B — Historical Reconstruction FIG 1.4-B ENIAC — 18,000 Vacuum Tubes, 1945
ENIAC (Electronic Numerical Integrator and Computer, 1945) — the first general-purpose electronic computer. Ten panels of vacuum tube circuitry filling a 30-metre room. 17,468 tubes, 70,000 resistors, 10,000 capacitors, consuming 150kW of power. One tube failed on average every two days, requiring a dedicated maintenance team. Despite this, ENIAC computed 5,000 additions per second — thousands of times faster than a human computer and 100× faster than a relay machine. It was programmed by physically rewiring patch cables between panels.
Type E — Before vs After FIG 1.4-C Relay vs Vacuum Tube — Five Dimensions of Comparison
Five-dimension comparison of relays vs vacuum tubes. The tube wins on switching speed (1000×) and electromagnetic noise immunity (no arcing contacts). The relay wins on reliability (filaments burn out) and power consumption (heaters draw significant current). Physical size is comparable — both are centimetre-scale. The transistor (Module 1.5) wins every dimension, which is why it replaced both within a decade of its invention.
