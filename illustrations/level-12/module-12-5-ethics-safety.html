Type A — Chapter Hero FIG 12.5-A AI Risk Taxonomy — Capability, Misuse, Accident, Alignment
AI risk taxonomy. Misuse risks arise from deliberate bad actors using AI capabilities for harm — generating disinformation, cyberattacks, fraud. Technical filters help but cannot fully prevent misuse of general-purpose systems. Alignment risks are more subtle: a powerful optimiser given the wrong objective will pursue it in unexpected ways — the famous "paperclip maximiser" thought experiment illustrates how an AI optimising for a simple goal could behave catastrophically. Accident risks are unintended harms from deployment errors, edge cases, or distributional shift. Fairness and bias risks arise from unrepresentative training data producing models that perform worse for underrepresented groups. These categories are not exclusive — many real harms involve multiple risk types simultaneously.
Type C — Process Diagram FIG 12.5-B Bias in Machine Learning — From Biased Data to Biased Decisions
ML bias pipeline — how societal inequalities enter, persist, and amplify through machine learning systems. Biased historical data (e.g., hiring records reflecting past discrimination) is collected non-uniformly (underrepresenting minorities), labelled by annotators with subconscious biases, used to train models that optimise average accuracy (performing worst on underrepresented groups), and deployed in high-stakes automated decision systems. A feedback loop compounds the problem: biased hiring decisions produce biased future employment records, which become biased future training data. Real cases: COMPAS recidivism algorithm (racially disparate predictions), Amazon's hiring tool (discriminated against women), facial recognition (higher error rates for darker skin). Auditing for disparate impact and applying fairness constraints are necessary but not sufficient — there is no single mathematical definition of "fairness" that satisfies all intuitive requirements simultaneously.
Type D — Process Diagram FIG 12.5-C Constitutional AI — RLHF and Self-Critique for Safer LLMs
Two major alignment techniques. RLHF (Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback) was the key innovation that transformed GPT-3 (capable but unreliable) into InstructGPT and eventually GPT-4. Human raters compare model outputs, a reward model learns their preferences, and Proximal Policy Optimisation (PPO) adjusts the model to generate higher-reward responses. A KL-divergence penalty prevents the model from deviating too far from its original capabilities. Constitutional AI (Anthropic) addresses RLHF's scaling bottleneck — human labelling is expensive. Instead: write explicit principles, have the model critique its own outputs against those principles, then use an AI model as the "human rater" (RLAIF). This enables alignment training to scale without proportional human annotation cost. Both approaches target the core alignment challenge: encoding human values into an optimisation objective without creating unexpected loopholes.
