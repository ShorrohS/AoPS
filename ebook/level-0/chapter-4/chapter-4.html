Babbage and Ada:
The First Programmers

The design of a general-purpose computer, a century before electronics

The Problem

You are an astronomer in 1820. You need a table of logarithms accurate to twenty decimal places. The only way to produce it: hire a room full of human computers to calculate each value by hand and have another room check their work.

Charles Babbage receives such a table, sits down to check it, and finds error after error. He slams it on the desk and says to his colleague:

“I wish to God these calculations had been executed by steam.”

That frustration launched forty years of obsessive work — and produced the first complete design of a general-purpose programmable computer, a century before electronics made it practical.

The Story

The Difference Engine — 1822

Babbage’s first machine, the Difference Engine, was designed to compute polynomial functions using the method of finite differences.

Almost every useful mathematical table — logarithms, trigonometric functions, astronomical positions — can be approximated by a polynomial. Polynomials have a beautiful property: if you take successive differences of their values, you eventually reach a constant. For f(n) = n² + n + 41 at n = 0, 1, 2, 3…: the values are 41, 43, 47, 53, 61, 71… The first differences are 2, 4, 6, 8, 10… The second differences are always 2.

So to compute the next value of f(n), you only ever need to add — no multiplication required. Babbage’s Difference Engine encoded this: columns of gear wheels, each representing one “order” of difference. Turn a crank; the next value computes automatically.

⊕ Historical Note

Babbage began construction in 1823 with British government funding. After a decade and £17,000 (millions in today’s money), the machine was abandoned. The manufacturing precision required was beyond what 1830s toolmaking could deliver. Parts didn’t fit. The project collapsed. A complete Difference Engine No. 2 was finally built by the Science Museum in London in 1991 using Babbage’s original drawings — and it worked perfectly.

The Analytical Engine — General-Purpose (1837 onward)

Before the Difference Engine was even finished, Babbage’s mind had already moved on to something far more ambitious: the Analytical Engine, a general-purpose mechanical computer.

Its design, completed in 1837, included:

Point five in the table is the key. A machine that can only follow a fixed sequence is a calculator. A machine that can decide — take this branch if the result is zero, that branch if it isn’t — is a computer. Babbage designed that decision in 1837. Every if statement you will ever write is the direct descendant of his conditional lever.

⊕ Historical Note

The Analytical Engine was never built. Babbage couldn’t raise funding, couldn’t get government support after the Difference Engine debacle, and spent his remaining years revising the design. He died in 1871 with the machine unbuilt. He left behind thousands of engineering drawings and the most sophisticated mechanical computing design in human history.

Ada Lovelace — The First Programmer

In 1842, an Italian mathematician named Luigi Menabrea published a paper describing the Analytical Engine in French, based on lectures Babbage gave in Turin. A young English mathematician — Augusta Ada King, Countess of Lovelace — was asked to translate it.

Ada did not just translate. She added her own Notes, labeled A through G, that were three times longer than the original paper.

Note G is the most famous document in the history of computing. It contains the first published algorithm intended to be executed by a machine: a procedure for computing Bernoulli numbers using the Analytical Engine. It demonstrates a complete understanding of what an algorithm is: a finite sequence of unambiguous steps that can be mechanically followed. And it contains a visionary observation: “The Engine might act upon other things besides number… the Engine might compose elaborate and scientific pieces of music of any degree of complexity or extent.”

Ada understood something Babbage never fully articulated: the Engine’s operations were not fundamentally about numbers — they were about symbols that could represent anything. This insight took the field another century to catch up to.

◆ Key Insight

Ada’s algorithm for Bernoulli numbers uses variables stored in the Store (memory locations), arithmetic operations in the Mill (the ALU), a looping structure (repeating a set of operations), and a sequence of operation cards (the program). It is, by every meaningful definition, a program. The person who wrote it was, by every meaningful definition, a programmer.

The Concept — Calculator vs Computer

The Difference Engine was not a computer. It was a sophisticated calculator — it could do one thing (polynomial tabulation) and could not be redirected to a different task.

The Analytical Engine was a computer. It had input, storage, processing, output, conditional execution, and loops. The decisive feature is conditional execution: the ability to take different actions based on the result of a computation. That is what separates a machine that follows a fixed path from a machine that can reason about its own output.

What “Algorithm” Means

Ada used this word — from al-Khwarizmi, the ninth-century Persian mathematician whose name gave us “algorithm” — to describe a precise, finite, unambiguous sequence of steps that can be followed mechanically to produce a result.

An algorithm is: finite (it terminates); precise (each step is unambiguous); general (it works for any valid input, not just one specific case); and effective (each step can actually be carried out). This definition has not changed in 185 years. Every function you will ever write is an algorithm.

“The Analytical Engine has no power of originating anything. It can only do what we know how to order it to perform.” — Ada Lovelace, Note G, 1843

Hands-On Exercise

The simulation implements a simplified virtual Analytical Engine with two tools: a Difference Engine that steps through polynomial calculations, and a minimal Analytical Engine emulator with a Store, Mill, and operation card editor. Ada’s Bernoulli algorithm is pre-loaded as an example.

▷ Step-by-Step Walkthrough

Step 1 — Difference Engine. Enter coefficients for f(n) = n² (a = 1, b = 0, c = 0). Step through the calculation. Observe that after two steps, the second differences are always 2 — the engine computes the next value using only addition from that point on.

Step 2 — Analytical Engine. The Store has 10 variables (V0–V9). Write a simple two-instruction program: ADD V0 V1 → V2 (add V0 and V1 and store in V2). Set V0=5, V1=7. Run. Verify V2=12.

Step 3. Observe the critical difference: the Difference Engine computed only what its initial setup determined. The Analytical Engine can be reprogrammed by changing the operation cards. That is the difference between a calculator and a computer.

Going Deeper

∇ Why the Analytical Engine Matters Despite Never Being Built

Babbage’s machine was never built. So why does it matter? First: it proved the concept was coherent. When the Science Museum built the Difference Engine from Babbage’s drawings in 1991, it worked. The Analytical Engine would have worked too — just extremely slowly and at enormous cost.

Second: it established the architecture. The Store + Mill + input/output + conditional branch is the Von Neumann architecture, a century before Von Neumann wrote it down. Every computer you have ever used is a descendant of this structure.

Third: Ada named the abstraction. By writing the first algorithm and articulating that the Engine worked on symbols (not just numbers), she defined programming as a discipline separate from hardware construction. The first person to understand what software is understood it in 1843, about a punch-card-driven mechanical engine that was never built.

Summary

Babbage designed a general-purpose computer in 1837. Ada programmed it in 1843. Neither could build it. But the ideas were complete.

Store, Mill, conditional branch, loop, output: these five concepts define a computer. Every processor since 1945 implements them. The names have changed. The architecture has not. When you write if, while, a variable assignment, and a function call, you are using Babbage’s Analytical Engine — built from silicon rather than brass, executing in nanoseconds rather than minutes, but structurally the same machine.
