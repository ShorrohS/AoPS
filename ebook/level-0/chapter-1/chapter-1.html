Why Did Humans
Need to Compute?

The problem that forced computing into existence

The Problem

Imagine you are the United States government in 1880. You need to count fifty million people — record their ages, occupations, birthplaces, and family sizes — accurately enough to redraw congressional districts and allocate federal funding.

You have pencils, paper, and humans.

It takes eight years.

By the time you finish, the next census is already two years away.

This was not a failure of effort. It was a failure of scale. Human brains are remarkable instruments, but they break down when asked to perform the same arithmetic operation millions of times without error.

That breakdown is exactly why computing had to be invented.

The Story

Problem One — The Census Crisis

The 1880 US Census employed hundreds of clerks who hand-tallied results on paper. The final count was published in 1888 — eight years after the census was taken.

The US Constitution requires a census every ten years. The arithmetic was alarming: the 1890 census was going to be even larger, and at the current rate, it might not be finished before the 1900 census began. Congress held a competition to find a faster method. A young engineer named Herman Hollerith won it — but that is Module 0.5's story.

The point here is simpler: large-scale counting broke human capacity.

⊕ Historical Note

The clerks employed on the 1880 census were not unskilled workers — many held degrees in mathematics. The problem was not human intelligence. It was human throughput: a trained clerk could process roughly 500 records per day. Fifty million records divided by 500 clerks processing 500 records each is 200 working days of pure counting — before verification, compilation, or printing. The eight-year figure reflects the full pipeline, not just the arithmetic phase.

Problem Two — Navigation and the Cost of Error

In the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, a ship's navigator relied on printed mathematical tables to determine position at sea. These tables — logarithms, trigonometric values, astronomical positions — were computed by hand and typeset by hand.

Hand computation means human error. Typesetting means a second layer of human error applied to the first.

In 1628, the Swedish warship Vasa sank on its maiden voyage, partly due to a design calculation error. Navigation errors sent ships onto rocks. Artillery fired with incorrect trajectory tables killed soldiers on the wrong side of the line.

The British government commissioned Mathematical Tables projects — employing teams of human "computers," people whose job title was literally computer — to calculate and cross-check these values. Errors still crept in.

⊕ Historical Note

The word computer referred to a human being long before it referred to a machine. The Oxford English Dictionary records its first use in this sense in 1613. For more than three centuries, a computer was a person — typically a mathematician or skilled arithmetician — employed to perform calculations. The shift in meaning happened gradually after World War Two, as electronic machines took over the role that humans had held.

Problem Three — Artillery and Wartime Calculation

During World War One and World War Two, artillery crews needed firing tables — books of pre-calculated values telling gunners how to aim based on distance, wind speed, shell weight, and air pressure. Each table required thousands of calculations.

At Aberdeen Proving Ground in the United States, teams of human computers — many of them women with mathematics degrees who could not obtain professional positions in academia or industry due to discrimination — spent days computing a single ballistic trajectory by hand. During World War Two, the demand for new firing tables outpaced what any number of humans could produce in the time available.

This bottleneck directly funded the construction of ENIAC — the first general-purpose electronic computer, completed in 1945. The machine that would change everything was built because human arithmetic had become a strategic military liability.

⊕ Historical Note

Six women programmed ENIAC: Kay McNulty, Betty Jennings, Betty Snyder, Marlyn Wescoff, Fran Bilas, and Ruth Lichterman. Their work — writing the sequences of operations that ENIAC would execute — was essential to the machine's function, and was not publicly credited for decades. They were referred to as "operators," a term that obscured the intellectual nature of what they did. Programming ENIAC required understanding its architecture at a physical level and constructing complex logical sequences entirely from first principles. There was no programming language yet. There was no debugger. There was only the machine and a problem to solve.

The Pattern

Three problems. Three different domains — government, navigation, warfare. One underlying structure.

Table 1.1
Problem Scale Bottleneck Consequence
Census counting 50,000,000 records 500 rec / clerk / day 8-year publication delay; stale, unusable data
Navigation tables Millions of ops Multi-step hand arithmetic Ships lost; lives and cargo destroyed
Artillery trajectories 1,000s of ballistic calc per table Days per trajectory Military disadvantage; strategic liability

Table 1.1 — Three historical problems that drove the development of computing. In every case, the task was well-defined and repetitive; the obstacle was human speed and reliability at scale.

◆ Key Insight

These three problems look like different domains. They share one structure: a task that is perfectly well-defined and mechanically repetitive, but too large in volume for humans to perform reliably at speed. Any time you encounter that structure, you are looking at a candidate for automation. This pattern recurs at every level of computing history — from census clerks to compilers to cloud infrastructure.

The Concept — What is Computation?

Computation, at its core, is the manipulation of symbols according to rules.

Symbol — any representation of information: a number, a letter, a hole in a card, a voltage in a wire.

Rule — a defined operation: add these two numbers, shift this value left, record a tally mark.

Manipulation — applying the rule to the symbol to produce a new symbol.

That is the entire definition. A human clerk doing long division is computing. A gear turning in a calculating machine is computing. A transistor switching in a chip is computing. The what is identical across all three. What changes is the medium — and with the medium, the speed, reliability, and cost.

Table 1.2
Medium Era Speed Error rate Tireless?
Human computer Pre-1642 ~500 ops / day 2–5% (fatigue) No
Mechanical calculator 1642–1890 ~1,000 ops / day Near-zero Yes
Electromechanical tabulator 1890–1945 31,200 ops / day Near-zero Yes
Electronic computer (ENIAC) 1945 5,000 ops / sec Near-zero Yes

Table 1.2 — The history of computing is largely the history of improving this table. Each row represents a different answer to the same underlying problem.

"The trajectory of computing history is a single unbroken line: making that table more favourable, row by row."

What "Stored Instructions" Means — A Preview

The next major leap was not just making machines faster. It was making them programmable.

A machine that can only add is useful but limited. A machine that can follow a sequence of instructions — and change what it does based on previous results — is general-purpose. The idea of storing a program alongside data, rather than hardwiring operations into the machine's physical structure, is the single most important idea in computing.

You will see it born in Module 0.3 with Jacquard's loom, formalised in Module 0.4 with Babbage's Analytical Engine, and industrialised in Module 0.5 with Hollerith's tabulating machine. For now, hold this idea lightly: the separation of instructions from mechanism is what makes a computer a computer rather than a calculator.

◇ Think About It

The three problems in this chapter — census, navigation, artillery — share an abstract structure: a large number of identical, well-defined operations that humans perform too slowly or too unreliably at scale.

Can you identify a modern task with the same structure that is not yet automated? What is the obstacle to automating it — is it technical, economic, or something else?

Read the walkthrough below, then open the simulation. Do not skip it. Reading about the tedium of manual tallying is not the same as experiencing it.

▷ Step-by-Step Walkthrough

Step 1. Open the simulation. You will see the Human Mode (1880) tab active.

Step 2. Read each record card and click it to process it. This represents the act of reading, categorising, and tallying one person's census record into the tally sheet below.

Step 3. After processing five or six records, click Inject Fatigue Error. Watch the tally sheet — one count is now wrong. This error is invisible in the records themselves.

Step 4. Process all twenty records, then click Submit Tally. Note your elapsed time and any discrepancies.

◆ What to Observe

The simulation compresses fifty million records into a few seconds of machine time. In reality, one hundred Hollerith tabulators running simultaneously took approximately six weeks. The point is not the exact speed — it is the order of magnitude. Six weeks versus eight years is not an optimisation. It is a phase transition: from data that arrives too late to be useful, to data that can influence the policy decisions of the Congress that commissioned it.

Going Deeper

∇ The Human Computers

Between 1930 and 1945, the US government's Mathematical Tables Project employed over 450 people in New York City as human computers, calculating logarithms, trigonometric functions, and other values needed for scientific and military work. The project was specifically designed to provide employment to mathematically skilled workers during the Depression. Many were Black men and women who could not obtain professional positions in academia due to segregation.

The parallel story of the Black women mathematicians employed as human computers at NACA — later NASA's Langley Research Center — is documented in Hidden Figures by Margot Lee Shetterly (2016). Their calculations were essential to every major flight programme of the early space age, including John Glenn's orbital mission. Glenn himself refused to fly until Katherine Johnson, a human computer, had verified the electronic computer's trajectory by hand.

→ Future Connection — Level 3: Machine Architecture

The demand for faster, more reliable computation that drove the construction of ENIAC did not stop when ENIAC was finished. ENIAC was still a specialised machine — to change what it computed, engineers physically rewired it. The next unsolved problem was how to make the program itself storable and changeable without hardware modification. That idea — the stored-program computer — is the subject of Level 3: Machine Architecture, Module 3.2: The Fetch-Decode-Execute Cycle.

Setup — Given Data

The 1880 US Census counted approximately 50,000,000 people. Each person's record included five data fields: name, age, occupation, birthplace, and family size.

A trained human computer in 1880 could process — read, categorise, tally, and record — roughly 500 records per day, working an eight-hour day.

The Hollerith tabulating machine (1890) could process roughly 65 cards per minute, running eight hours a day.

Summary

Three problems. One pattern.

Every major pressure that drove the invention of computing — census tabulation, navigation tables, artillery firing tables — was a problem of scale meeting repetition. Humans are capable of any individual arithmetic step. What humans cannot do is perform that step millions of times, in sequence, without accumulating error or slowing to a stop.

The solution was always the same direction: offload the repetition to a mechanism. That impulse — to build a machine that will do the counting so humans can do the thinking — is the seed from which every computer in history grew.
